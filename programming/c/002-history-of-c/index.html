The Story of C — Click Any Event

Tap any milestone to expand its story. Follow the journey from a failed project to the world's most influential language.

1969
Bell Labs Pulls Out of Multics
Click to expand →
Bell Labs was a partner in the Multics project with MIT and GE. When it became too expensive and slow, they pulled out. Ken Thompson, a researcher there, began looking for alternatives — something simpler, something he could build himself.
1969
Ken Thompson Creates B
Click to expand →
Thompson tried Fortran first but quickly abandoned it. Instead, he created a stripped-down version of BCPL (a language from Martin Richards) and called it B. It was typeless — everything was just a "word" in memory. B was used to write early Unix utilities but had limitations.
1971
"New B" (NB) — A Step Toward C
Click to expand →
Bell Labs acquired a PDP-11 computer. B couldn't take advantage of its byte-addressable memory or handle floating-point numbers. Ritchie began extending B by adding a char type and rewriting the compiler to generate real machine code. He called it NB, for "new B."
1972
The Language Gets Its Name: C
Click to expand →
Ritchie added structures, the && and || operators, and a preprocessor. NB no longer felt like "new B" — it was its own thing. He named it C, following the single-letter tradition. The name could mean a progression through the alphabet, or through the letters in BCPL.
1973
Unix Kernel Rewritten in C
Click to expand →
The Unix kernel — the core of the operating system — was rewritten in C. This was a huge moment: it proved that C was powerful enough to build an entire operating system. No more writing everything in assembly language. C was ready for real work.
1978
The K&R Book Is Published
Click to expand →
Brian Kernighan and Dennis Ritchie published The C Programming Language. This book — often called K&R or the "white book" — became the de facto standard for C. For over a decade, it was the only real reference anyone had.
1983–1989
ANSI Standardization
Click to expand →
By the early 1980s, C was being used everywhere — but different compilers behaved slightly differently. ANSI formed a committee (X3J11) to create an official standard. In 1989, ANSI C was ratified. In 1990, it became an ISO international standard (C90). This is the foundation of the C we write today.
1999–Today
C99, C11, C17, C23 — C Keeps Evolving
Click to expand →
C has been updated many times. C99 added new types and features. C11 added multi-threading support. C17 was a cleanup. C23, released in 2024, is the latest standard. Over 50 years after its creation, C is still one of the most used languages in the world.

Fun Facts

Did You Know?

💡The Name "C"

Nobody is 100% sure why it's called C. Ritchie said it could mean the next letter after B, or the next letter in BCPL. He left it open on purpose.

⌨️The ++ Operator

Thompson invented ++ and -- for B. He noticed that translating ++x produced smaller code than x=x+1. Efficiency drove everything.

📖The White Book

The K&R book was so influential that for over 10 years, it was the only real specification of C. There was no official standard until 1989.

🏗️Built on a Tiny Machine

C was created on a PDP-7 with only 8K words of memory — less than a modern email. It was designed to fit in extremely tight constraints.

Recap

What You Just Learned

You explored the entire history of C — from a failed project to the world's most influential programming language. Here's the summary:

Origin
C was born at Bell Labs alongside Unix, created by Dennis Ritchie between 1969–1973.
Evolution
BCPL → B → NB → C. Each step added types, speed, and expressiveness.
Standardization
ANSI C (1989) and ISO C (1990) unified the language. C99, C11, and C23 followed.
Legacy
C influenced C++, Java, Python, Go, Rust — and still powers operating systems today.
